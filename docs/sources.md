# Sources

Status: complete. All six sources were opened and read on 2026-11-18. For the three PDFs I extracted the full text and read the passages cited. For the three web pages I downloaded the page and checked the quoted phrases in the page text. Search snippets were not used as evidence.

These sources give public context on the Terwilliger Center for Innovation in Shelter (TCIS) and on housing microfinance. They say nothing about how TCIS or Habitat run any internal process, and the case study makes no such claim.

## The six sources

### S1. Terwilliger Center for Innovation in Shelter (Habitat for Humanity)
- URL: https://www.habitat.org/our-work/terwilliger-center-innovation-in-shelter
- Type: organisation web page.
- **Supports:** what TCIS is and what it works on. It is "a specialized unit within Habitat influencing and strengthening housing systems worldwide", established in 2016 through a gift from J. Ron Terwilliger. The page lists housing finance (the Center helps financial institutions "design and scale loan and insurance products tailored to how low-income families actually build", which it describes as "incrementally and often with irregular incomes"), building materials and technologies, design and construction services, policy and regulation, and knowledge and information.
- Caveat: the page lists five focus areas. The job brief names three (housing finance, construction products and services, enabling policy), so use the page's own words if the case study lists areas.
- Caveat: the page gives scale figures ("49 million people" since 2016) with no as-of date. The case study should not quote them.

### S2. Housing microfinance (Habitat for Humanity, TCIS)
- URL: https://www.habitat.org/our-work/terwilliger-center-innovation-in-shelter/microfinance
- Type: organisation web page.
- **Supports:** the definition of housing microfinance as "small, non-mortgage backed loans starting at just a few hundred dollars" for incremental building, and the statement that building in stages is "a common practice in much of the developing world, constituting 50 to 90 percent of residential construction". It says Habitat "does not issue microfinance loans itself" and works with partner microfinance institutions through its MicroBuild Fund.
- Caveat: the page does **not** describe how lenders assess applicants. Do not cite it for assessment practice. Use S3 for that.

### S3. Housing Microfinance Product Development: A Handbook, Part 1 (3rd edition, 2015)
- URL: https://www.habitat.org/sites/default/files/15.45203.HMF_.Handbook_P1.MNL_.pdf
- Type: handbook, 64 pages. Published by Habitat for Humanity International, Center for Innovation in Shelter and Finance. Editors Sandra Prieto and Sheldon Yoder.
- **Supports:**
  - How loans are approved (page 18): the approval process "is generally based on an assessment of the applicant's ability to pay (cash flow) and assumed willingness to pay (character assessment)".
  - Why documents alone are not enough (page 18): customers "generally participate in the informal economy and do not have steady and verifiable income, additional analysis is used to evaluate the creditworthiness of a borrower". Page 25 adds that informal workers are generally excluded from commercial housing finance "because of a lack of verifiable, stable, official income or land title".
  - The checking steps that surround a loan (pages 45 and 46): "pre-disbursement due diligence" can include budget verification, visiting prospective applicants to review proposed home improvements, preparing cost estimates, "verifying cost estimates with the approved loan amount" and "verifying the land title (or land security status) of the applicant".
- Caveats: it is a 2015 document from the unit then named Center for Innovation in Shelter and Finance. I have not verified how that unit relates to TCIS, so the case study cites it by its own name. A statistic on page 18 (63 percent of surveyed institutions require a project budget) comes from a 2014 survey whose size I could not find. I do not plan to use it.

### S4. Nyumba Smart Loans (Habitat for Humanity, TCIS, with MasterCard Foundation)
- URL: https://www.habitat.org/sites/default/files/TCIS-MCF-Nyumba-smart-loans.pdf
- Type: four-page project note. The text reports data to June 2017 and the file was produced in August 2017. The document carries no printed date, so the case study should say "2017".
- **Supports:** a Kenyan example of a housing microfinance product at scale. TCIS gave technical assistance to Kenya Women Microfinance Bank (KWFT) for a product branded Nyumba Smart Loan. KWFT disbursed on average 1,600 loans a month, and by the end of June 2017 more than 38,000 loans had been made through over 200 branches. Most loans were small: 73 percent were below KSH100,000 (about US$964 at the time). Local artisans called fundis did over 90 percent of the improvements and "ultimately determined the cost and timing". The note also says "a lending process made easy for KWFT and clients allowed KWFT to expand the product quickly", with terms and processes "very similar to those of other KWFT loans, which make them easy for both credit officers and clients to understand".
- Caveat: 2017, one lender, one country. Use it as an example only.

### S5. Building value in microfinance through digitization: Lessons from loan automation pilots (CGAP, November 2023)
- URL: https://cgap.org/sites/default/files/publications/FocusNote_DigitizationLessons.pdf
- Type: CGAP focus note, 34 pages. Authors Mark Flaming, Ivo Jeník, Isabelle Barrès and Khin Moet Moet Nyein. Licence CC BY 4.0.
- **Supports:**
  - Loan officer time is the scaling constraint. Table 1 (page 12) models a loan officer who originates five first loans and ten follow-up loans a month. That takes 16.3 days of an average 19 days of monthly net capacity, or 86 percent. The note states that "the value of automation derives directly from an increase in loan officer productivity".
  - Origination for new borrowers is labour-heavy. One lender wanted to free officers for "the more labor-intensive first-time borrower loan origination". The note describes microfinance institutions' strength as "high-touch loan origination, especially for first-time borrowers".
  - Human control in automated decisions. In the pilots, credit officers could disqualify a client from an automated offer through an administration portal, which gave them "adequate control over clients who they know are struggling". To build staff confidence, the first tests checked that the model "is applying the same rules that they apply during appraisal".
  - A regulatory check can force a slow on-site visit. Where a creditworthiness test can only be done on site, "the onsite visit represents a significant slowdown".
- **Caveat: Table 1 is a modelling scenario with assumed inputs, not a measurement.** Quote it as "a CGAP model", never as an observed result. The pilots automated loan **renewals** with a scoring service, so they relate to this case study by analogy only. The note also reports that the prototype "generally did not reduce the total workload of the loan officers".

### S6. Can Platform Data Expand Access to Transformative Credit? (CGAP blog, 8 June 2022)
- URL: https://www.cgap.org/blog/can-platform-data-expand-access-to-transformative-credit
- Type: blog post by Malika Anand, CGAP.
- **Supports:** why income from informal work is hard to assess. Lenders "are unable to accurately assess the credit worthiness of microenterprises and informal laborers since they lack assets and earn and save in cash without corresponding transaction records". As a result these segments "only qualify for small loans".
- Caveat: the post is about platform and gig data, not housing loans. Use it for the narrow point about cash income and missing records.

## What these sources do not support

- Any statement about TCIS staff, TCIS partners' internal processes, or how any lender uses documents today beyond what S3 and S4 say.
- A measured time per loan file. S5 gives a model, and no source here gives an observed time for document-based assessment in housing microfinance.
- Anything about AI use at Habitat or at housing lenders.
- Anything about Kenyan data protection law. If the design section cites consent and data minimisation as legal requirements, a verified source is needed for that first, and none is recorded here.
